Should Schools Start Later? The Case for Letting Teenagers Sleep

Every weekday at 6:15 a.m., my alarm drags me out of a sleep my body insists is not finished. I am not lazy; I am biologically a teenager. Research on adolescent sleep shows that during puberty the body releases melatonin roughly two hours later than it does in children or adults, which means most teenagers cannot fall asleep before 11 p.m. no matter how early they go to bed. Schools that start before 8:30 a.m. are therefore asking students to learn during what is, for their brains, the middle of the night. School districts should move high school start times to 8:30 a.m. or later, because doing so improves health, academic performance, and safety.

The strongest reason is health. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that teenagers get eight to ten hours of sleep, yet surveys consistently find that most high school students get fewer than eight on school nights. Chronic sleep loss is linked to depression, weakened immune systems, and weight gain. When a student loses an hour of sleep every day, the deficit does not disappear on the weekend; it accumulates. A later start time does not ask students to change their biology, it simply stops punishing them for it.

Later start times also lead to better learning. When districts in Minnesota pushed their high school start times back by about an hour, researchers found that attendance rose and the number of students falling asleep in class dropped. Some schools also reported modest gains in grades in first-period classes, which makes sense: a student who is awake can actually absorb the lesson on the board instead of merely copying it. If the goal of school is learning, then scheduling classes for the hours when students' brains are most alert is not a luxury but common sense.

Finally, there is the issue of safety. Teenage drivers are already the most at-risk group on the road, and drowsy driving makes the danger worse. After one Wyoming district delayed its start time, car crashes involving teen drivers fell noticeably. Fewer exhausted sixteen-year-olds behind the wheel at dawn is a benefit that extends to everyone sharing the road with them.

Critics argue that later start times would disrupt after-school sports, part-time jobs, and bus schedules, and these concerns are real. However, many districts that made the change adjusted practice schedules and staggered bus routes with fewer problems than expected. More importantly, a schedule built around convenience for adults should not outweigh the well-being of the students the system exists to serve. Logistics can be redesigned; a teenager's circadian rhythm cannot.

Starting school later is not about pampering teenagers. It is about aligning education with what science tells us about how young people's bodies work. Healthier, more alert, and safer students are worth a few rearranged bus routes. It is time for school boards to let teenagers sleep, and then let them learn.
